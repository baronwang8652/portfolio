About

I came to design sideways, and it turned out to be the useful direction.

A degree in information management, then two years as a system analyst, then three designing a B2B e-signature platform. The order matters — by the time I was designing interfaces, I already knew what it costs to build one.

How I got here

I studied Industrial and Information Management at National Cheng Kung University — a degree about using software to take management work off people's hands. Becoming a system analyst after that was a short step: sit with a client, work out how their operation actually runs, and design the features that fit it.

Somewhere in the middle of that I noticed which part of the job I cared about. Not the requirements document. The moment someone opened the thing we'd built and it made sense to them without being explained. Making software that is genuinely good to use is the part I find worth the effort, and it's why I moved from analysis into design.

The bet I'm making

Designers who arrive at UX from a non-technical route get asked the same question over and over, usually politely: can you design something that can actually be built?

I came the other way round. I spent two years writing specifications engineers built directly from, drawing UML, working in SQL, and taking part in architecture planning for cloud and on-premise deployments. So I know roughly what a permission model costs, why a framework sometimes can't insert a step in the middle of a flow, and what happens to a design when it meets an API that won't bend.

That isn't a claim to be an engineer. It's a claim that I rarely design something a team then has to talk me out of — and that when a constraint does bite, I can usually tell whether it's a real one.

Why Canada

I'll be straight about it. UX in Taiwan is often under-valued — at some companies the discipline barely has a seat at the table, and that shows up in what the work is paid.

I wanted to work somewhere the argument is about whether a design is right, not about whether design is worth doing. So I moved to Ottawa and went back to school to make the switch properly rather than half-way.

Now

I'm studying Interactive Media Design at Algonquin College, graduating in 2028, and looking for a product design internship starting Winter 2027, ahead of my program's co-op term in Fall 2027.

I'm most useful on B2B products with real operational complexity behind them — the sort where the interesting problems live in permission models, sequencing, edge cases, and what the product does when something goes wrong. If that's what you're building, I'd like to hear about it.
